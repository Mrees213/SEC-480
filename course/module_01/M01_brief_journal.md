## What the technical journal is

You keep one per class, across your years in this program. It sits between a scratchpad and a
formal technical journal. Syntax notes belong in it. So do reflections, dead ends, and half
-formed analysis. It is not a polished document and it is not meant to become one.

The reason it exists is retention. Writing down what you just did, in your own words, while it
is still warm, is one of the few study habits with real evidence behind it. The second reason is
that it makes your thinking visible, which is how this course grades process alongside product.

## What changes in this course

In most classes the journal is a log. Here it starts as a log and becomes a reflection.

Through the first unit you are mostly writing up what you did and what happened. From the second
week onward the entries shift toward judgment: what worked, what the platform got wrong, what
you would do differently. That shift is deliberate and it is where most of the value is, because
a log of steps is reproducible from the lab sheet anyway. Your assessment of those steps is not.

## Part one: where you are starting from

Four questions, before anything about the setup. There is no survey for module 1, so this is
where the course finds out who it is talking to, and your answers shape the examples you get for
the rest of the term.

Nobody else reads these. There is no right answer to any of them and a negative opinion is worth
as much as a positive one, probably more.

1. **How much have you worked with AI so far?** A few sentences on what you have actually done
   with it, not what you have read about.

   Then list the tools and platforms you have used, **one per line**, in this shape, so I can
   read them without guessing:

   ```
   - Claude: weekly, mostly for coursework
   - ChatGPT: tried it twice, did not stick
   - Copilot: daily, in VS Code
   ```

   If the honest answer is none, write `- None` and move on. That is a completely reasonable
   place to start this course from and it will not cost you anything.

2. **What do you like about AI, and why?** The why is the part I am asking for. "It is fast" is a
   start; what did it make faster, and did that turn out to matter?

3. **What do you not like about AI, and why?** Same standard. Be blunt. If you think the whole
   thing is oversold, say that and say what convinced you. This course is not going to argue you
   out of a position you can defend, and a room where everyone is enthusiastic is a worse room to
   learn critique in.

4. **How might you use AI for your capstone?** An unformed idea is a useful answer. So is "no
   idea yet." So is "I would rather not." If you already know your capstone direction, say what
   it is, because that is the single most useful thing you can tell me for tailoring examples.

## Part two: this module's work

Write these five. Headings, a few sentences each.

1. **What I set up.** Repository, access, integration. Plain description.
2. **What took more than one attempt.** Something did. Write down what it was, what you saw,
   and what you changed to get past it. If everything landed first try, write down the step you
   were least sure about and why.
3. **What surprised me.** One thing. It can be small. It can be that nothing was surprising,
   as long as you say what you had expected instead.
4. **My usage window numbers.** The figures you noted in `usage-window.md`, plus one
   sentence on what you think will happen to them as the term goes on.
5. **Three questions it could not answer.** Ask Claude three things it gets wrong or invents.
   Note the questions, what it said, and your read on whether you could have caught the
   invented ones without already knowing the answer. That last part is the point: an invention
   you can only spot because you already knew is not one you would have caught in the wild.

## How long this should take

Part one is opinion and needs no research, so it moves quickly. Part two is short by design. If
you find yourself writing an essay, you have gone past what is being asked for.

## How this gets graded

Not on writing quality. Not on whether things went smoothly. Not, in part one, on your opinions,
which cannot be wrong.

What earns credit is specificity. "The connector did not work, then it did" tells nobody
anything, including you in three weeks when you hit the same problem. "Claude answered about my
repository without being able to quote the README, so I asked for the exact text and found the
integration was pointed at a different repository" is the entry that is worth having written.

Write for yourself in three weeks. That person has forgotten the details and needs them.
